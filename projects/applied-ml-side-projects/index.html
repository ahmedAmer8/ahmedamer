Side projects, honestly scoped

Five applied-ML builds from before and alongside the production work. They were good for learning the tools and they were not production systems. Both halves of that are worth saying.

2024 — 2025Sole author archived 3 min

Most portfolios present work like this as though it were equivalent to production engineering. It is not, and pretending otherwise is the fastest way to lose the trust of anyone who has done both. So: here is what these were, and what I actually got out of them.

What they are

Multi-agent content generation — a researcher agent and a writer agent coordinated through an orchestration framework to produce an article from a topic. Streamlit front-end, search API for retrieval.

Chat with your PDFs — retrieval-augmented generation over a document collection, with a vector index and an LLM answering from retrieved chunks.

Voice translation with cloning — English speech in, six languages out, preserving the speaker’s voice, by chaining a transcription API and a synthesis API.

Medical image chat — a vision-language model answering questions about an uploaded image, with selectable backends and a chat interface.

Groq chatbot — a chat application with a separate API backend and front-end, containerised and deployed, with conversation history and token accounting.

What they actually taught me

The useful thing was not the models. It was everything around them.

Containerising the chatbot and having it fail in deployment was worth more than the chatbot. The difference between “works on my machine” and “works in a container somebody else runs” is where most of the real content is, and I would not have learned it by reading.

Token accounting in the Groq project turned out to be direct preparation for building a cost model over hundreds of thousands of production usage rows — including the instinct to record quantities rather than computed costs, though I did not know why that mattered until much later.

The RAG project taught me how retrieval fails, which is more useful than knowing how it works. It fails by returning plausible, adjacent, wrong chunks, and the answer built on them reads exactly like an answer built on correct ones. That is the same failure mode that produced an agent confidently selling a promotion that did not exist — the model does not signal when its inputs are bad.

What they did not teach me

Everything that only appears under real use.

There was no concurrency, because there was one user. No multi-tenancy, no data isolation, no question of whose data this is. No latency budget, because nobody was waiting in a queue behind anyone else. No failure direction to decide, because the only person a failure affected was me, and I could restart it. No deployment drift, no schema migrations against live data, no incident where the instrument was the thing that was broken.

Most importantly: no consequence for being wrong. A demo that hallucinates is a funny screenshot. An ordering agent that hallucinates is a customer waiting for food that is never coming, and I did not understand the difference between those until I had caused the second one.

Side projects teach you the tools. They cannot teach you the failure modes, because the failure modes are properties of scale, concurrency, and consequence — and a project with one user has none of the three.

Why they are still here

Because the progression is the point of this site. These are where I learned to build the things, and the production work is where I learned what building them actually costs. Presenting only the second half would suggest I arrived knowing it.

What was mine

Written entirely by me, for learning. None of them carried traffic from anyone who was not me.
