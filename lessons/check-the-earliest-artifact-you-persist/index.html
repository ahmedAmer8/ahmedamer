I was wrong three times before I asked the right question

Messages were disappearing. Three careful hypotheses, all disproven, before a single query settled it. The generalisable move is asking what the earliest thing you write down is, before your own logic can discard anything.

fixed methodology14 August 2026 3 min

Three wrong answers

Messages were not being replied to. I had three hypotheses, in order, each reasonable and each supported by something:

  1. The queue is losing them. Plausible — there had been a redelivery incident before. Disproven by the queue metrics.
  2. The platform is not calling us. Plausible — configuration drifts, tokens expire, apps sit in development mode. Disproven, eventually, but it took longest because our own runbook recommends it.
  3. A worker is crashing mid-turn. Plausible — an out-of-memory kill would produce exactly this shape, with the message redelivered and lost again. Disproven by the absence of restarts.

All three were careful. All three were wrong. The fourth thing I did took one query.

What the queue metrics said

This is the part worth keeping, because the metrics were not broken. They were accurate and they were irrelevant.

sent      32
received  32
deleted   32
dead-letter queue  empty

Thirty-two in, thirty-two out, nothing parked. A perfect record — of the messages that made it as far as the queue. The discard was happening inside the consuming process, after the message had been received and before anything was written down. From the queue’s point of view that is indistinguishable from successful processing, because the process did acknowledge it. It just did not do anything first.

“Received and deleted” means a process took responsibility for a message. It does not mean the message produced an effect. Those are different claims, and only the second one is what you care about.

The question that worked

Eventually I asked something different from “where is it going.” I asked:

What is the earliest thing we write down, before any of our own logic could throw it away?

For us that is a deduplication row, written on receipt of the webhook and before routing. It exists specifically so a redelivered message is not processed twice, which means it is written unconditionally and early — which in turn makes it, accidentally, the best diagnostic surface in the system.

The rows were there. With an empty sender field. That single result eliminated all three hypotheses at once and pointed straight at a bare continue in our own parser.

Why I had not asked it sooner

Because the shape of the problem suggested the boundaries. When input goes missing, attention goes to the edges — the network, the platform, the queue — and not to the code that runs after the input has safely arrived. The mental model was “something between them and us.” The reality was “something in us, immediately.”

An early unconditional write breaks that framing, because it is inside our system but before our interpretation. It answers “did it reach us” without depending on anything that could have discarded it.

The number that reframed the priority

The loss was about 0.06% of traffic.

That is below every alarm threshold we had, and it would have stayed below them indefinitely. It was also, for the roughly twenty people a day it affected, a total failure — they messaged a restaurant and got nothing. A rate that rounds to zero globally is not a small problem for anyone inside it.

A percentage is the wrong unit for a failure that is total for the affected user. Count the people, not the ratio.

What I do now

When something is missing, before forming hypotheses, I ask what the earliest persisted artifact is and whether it exists for the case in question. It is one query and it partitions the entire problem space in half: if the row is there, the fault is ours and downstream; if it is not, the fault is upstream.

Three careful hypotheses cost a day. That question costs a minute, and I should have spent the minute first.

The principle

When input goes missing, query the earliest artifact you persist before business logic runs. Everything downstream cannot distinguish "never arrived" from "arrived and was discarded."
