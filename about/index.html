About

I am an AI engineer in Cairo. I work on systems where a model's output becomes somebody's order, which changes what "good enough" means.

What I actually do

I work on a multi-tenant conversational commerce platform. Customers order food by chatting on WhatsApp; an agent handles the conversation, builds the cart, takes payment, and pushes the order into the restaurant's point-of-sale system. Several established food and beverage brands run on it, and the orders it creates are real orders that real kitchens cook.

That last part is the whole job. A recommendation engine that is wrong 5% of the time is a tuning problem. An ordering agent that is wrong 5% of the time is a refund, an angry customer, and a restaurant that stops trusting the product. Most of what I have learned comes from that gap.

How I work

I have converged on a small number of habits, all of which I arrived at by getting them wrong first.

Measure the thing, do not reason about the thing. I have written documents full of confident estimates about where time was going, then built the instrumentation and found that two of my conclusions were inverted. The estimates were not lazy — they were careful and wrong. Now I instrument first and argue second, and when I publish a number I say how it was measured.

Suspect the instrument. A surprising share of my worst bugs were in the measurement, not the system: a dashboard that overstated spend by 3.8×, a verification command that failed on correct data and overwrote the rows it was checking, queue counters that read healthy while messages were being discarded in-process. When a number is surprising, the first hypothesis should be that the number is wrong.

Decide who a failure is open for. Every guard fails somehow, and the direction is a design decision rather than an accident. The rule I ended up with: things on the customer's path fail open — a broken classifier lets the message through — and things that verify correctness fail closed, with a non-zero exit that breaks the build. Never block a customer; always break a build. Almost every serious incident I have worked on came from getting that direction backwards.

Write down what you believed. I keep a record of wrong hypotheses, not just fixes. The investigation is usually more reusable than the patch — the patch is specific to one bug, but "check the earliest artifact you persist before business logic can discard it" applies to the next one too.

What interests me

Systems where a probabilistic component sits inside a transactional one. That is the actual difficulty in most applied AI work: the model is not the hard part, the seam is. Where do you let the model decide, where do you make it prove its claim against a database, and what happens when the two disagree? I have spent a lot of time on that boundary and I still find it interesting.

Also: latency as a design constraint rather than an optimisation phase, evaluation that tells you something you did not already believe, and the specific discipline of instrumenting a system you did not design.

What I am learning

Infrastructure, mostly by necessity. I came in through models and found that the interesting failures were in queues, deployment, and process lifecycle. I have been working through distributed systems material and, more usefully, through the consequences of my own architecture decisions six months later.

I am also trying to get better at the part I am weakest at: knowing when a problem is worth the depth I want to give it.

Before this

Computer science at Cairo University, finished 2025. Before the current work I built the usual set of applied-ML side projects — retrieval over documents, multi-agent orchestration, speech translation, vision-language chat. They were genuinely useful for learning the tools and genuinely not production systems, and this site tries to be clear about which is which.

I taught machine learning sessions for IEEE for a year, which remains the fastest way I know to discover what you do not understand.
