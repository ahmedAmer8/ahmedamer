Robusta Technology Group

Joined as an AI intern to work on generative AI use cases. Fourteen months later the work is production systems engineering, and most of what I learned came from things breaking in ways I had not imagined.

Robusta Technology Group2025 —

I joined as an AI intern in July 2025 and now work as an AI engineer on the same product. The title changed; more usefully, what I think the job is changed.

The product is a multi-tenant conversational commerce platform: customers order food over WhatsApp, an agent handles the conversation and takes payment, and the order appears in the restaurant’s point-of-sale system. Several established food and beverage brands run on it.

It is a team product with a team architecture, led by our AI manager. What follows is scoped to what I owned or found, and where something was a collective design I have tried to say so rather than absorb it.

01

Arriving as an intern on a live product

I was brought in for generative AI work and put on a platform that was already carrying real orders for real restaurants. There is no gentle version of that. The first thing I had to unlearn was treating the model as the interesting part.

Almost everything I was asked to fix in the first months turned out to be ordinary engineering wearing an AI costume — a query in a loop, a cache invalidation that never fired, a scope missing from a database filter. The model was rarely the defect. It was frequently the symptom, because a model given bad inputs produces confident nonsense rather than an exception, and confident nonsense does not page anyone.

A menu that cost 19,369 queries

02

Learning that a guard can be the incident

I worked on the validation layer that checks an agent’s order before it is created. It was built to stop the agent confirming orders it had never placed, which was a real problem — fourteen customers had been told an order was confirmed when none existed.

Then I measured it, and it was rejecting two-thirds of legitimate orders. Its 143 unit tests passed because they stubbed the exact model call that was broken.

This changed how I think about safety mechanisms more than anything else I have worked on. A guard is not free, its failures are usually invisible, and the ones pointed at customers need a quantified false-positive rate before they ship.

The guard blocked more orders than the bug it was built to stop

03

Building the instruments

I had written a careful latency analysis reconstructed from log timestamps. When I built the actual tracing — a span for every model call, tool, outbound request and prompt build — two of that document’s recommendations turned out to be inverted, and the single largest cost in the system had not appeared in it at all.

I built the cost model the same way and found we had been overstating spend by 3.8× for eight months.

What I took from this is narrower than “measure things.” It is that a measurement needs a second, independent path that can disagree with it. Both systems now have a command that recomputes their headline number from raw data and fails a build on a discrepancy.

I wrote a careful latency analysis, then measured it and two conclusions flipped

04

Moving work off the customer's path

I contributed to the platform’s architecture moving from one shared worker pool to tiers: a thin ingress function that acknowledges the platform in about 25 milliseconds, one isolated queue that carries only the customer conversation, and separate compute for everything else.

The design decision I keep returning to is a refusal rather than a feature. There is deliberately no fallback from the new paths back onto the old worker. If the task queue is misconfigured, dispatch raises. A silent fallback would have reintroduced exactly the load the split exists to remove, and nobody would have noticed until latency drifted back.

Its counterpart matters equally: two operations that used to be queued tasks are now plain function calls, because a queue hop cost more than the work did.

05

Finding a platform change nobody had announced

WhatsApp began rolling out usernames. Senders who adopted one arrived with no phone number in the payload at all, and our ingest discarded those messages with a bare continue — no log, no row, no alert. About twenty people a day across seven tenants were messaging restaurants and getting silence.

I found it, established the scope against production data, verified the platform’s actual behaviour by probing the API rather than reading the documentation, and built the identity resolution.

The habit it left me with is a diagnostic one: when input goes missing, query the earliest artifact you persist before your own logic can discard anything. Everything downstream cannot distinguish “never arrived” from “arrived and was thrown away.”

Meta stopped sending the phone number, and we dropped the message silently

06

What changed about how I work

I came in thinking the hard part of applied AI was the model. The hard part is the seam — where a probabilistic component sits inside a transactional one, and you have to decide what it is allowed to assert versus what it has to prove.

Three habits came out of that, and they are the ones I would keep in any job. Decide a guard’s failure direction before writing it, and say so in the code. Put a constraint in something that refuses — a build, a deploy, a plan — rather than in a document. And never trust a test that stubs the component under test.
